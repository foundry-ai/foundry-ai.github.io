***Can artificial intelligence be relied upon?***

This question is at the heart of societal concerns regarding the widespread use of AI, and for good reason. On the one hand, the striking successes of AI cannot be questioned or ignored: we have very recently witnessed elaborate AI models breaking new ground and achieving feats previously thought impossible, from resolving open math problems of Clay Millennium fame, to protein folding and running entire codebases. At the same time, the very systems that ushered in the deep learning revolution can—and often *do*—fail in spectacular and unforeseen ways, from single-pixel attacks fooling deep networks to AI systems breaking out of their guardrails and operating in a completely unpredictable manner. And while it may not be critical if a movie recommendation or an automatic translation is not that accurate, the results can be downright devastating if an AI algorithm performs poorly in an autonomous car, an operating room, a power management facility, or other high-stakes application.

Invariably, these failures reflect an inherent lack of ***robustness***, a desideratum that has eluded the field since its earliest stages, and which has grown exponentially in urgency as machine learning technologies are now deployed “in vivo”. In particular, even though we are currently able to engineer systems that perform extremely well on a wide array of tasks, the resulting models tend to have a narrow flight envelope, being brittle, data-hungry, and unable to adapt reliably to situations that deviate from their training configuration. On that account, the overarching aim of the FOUNDRY proposal is *to develop the technical and theoretical* ***foundations of robustness and reliability in machine learning*** *that are needed to build and instill trust in current and emerging AI models and methodologies*.

In this context, it has become clear that trustworthiness and reliability in AI cannot be achieved by constructing a full world model: any principled path to robustness must go beyond the “business as usual” view of ML as a process for building predictive models with the highest possible accuracy. As such, we intend to focus on the following inter-connected challenges:

1. *Resilience to data-centric impediments (the “known unknowns”)*, i.e., learning with incomplete, corrupted and/or poisoned data, resistance to adversarial attacks, etc.
2. *Adaptivity to unmodeled phenomena and the environment itself (the “unknown unknowns”)*, i.e., maintaining optimal performance in non-stationary environments, in the presence of other agents, etc.
3. *Robustness in the presence of concurrent aims and goals*, i.e., when the performance of a model or algorithm must be balanced against several desiderata (distributional robustness, privacy, fairness, etc.).

These objectives form the principal agenda of the FOUNDRY consortium, which comprises six research teams based in Grenoble, Lyon, Paris, and Lille, and whose expertise spans the interface of machine learning, optimization, game theory, statistics, and information theory.
